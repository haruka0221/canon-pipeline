## Work identity and granularity policy

Apply the following rules when deciding whether a Wikidata item represents the target work.

1. Single underlying work
If the target is a translation, reprint, ordinary edited edition, or other bibliographic manifestation of a single identifiable literary work, resolve it to the underlying work-level item rather than to the edition-level item.

2. Compound or collected volumes
If the target combines two or more independently identifiable works (for example, "Work A and Work B"), do not arbitrarily select one constituent work.
Return NO_MATCH unless Wikidata contains a work-level item specifically representing that combined work or collection.

3. Abridgments, retellings, and adaptations
Do not automatically map an abridgment, retelling, dramatization, rewritten version, or other substantially transformed derivative work to its source work.
If the derivative has its own work-level item, select that item.
If only the source work exists, return NO_MATCH rather than collapsing the derivative into the source.

4. Edited or adapted editions
Words such as "edited by" or "adapted and edited by" do not by themselves establish a distinct work.
If the evidence indicates that the target is still bibliographically treated as the same underlying literary work, normalize to the underlying work.
If the adaptation constitutes an independently identifiable derivative work, apply Rule 3.

5. Part versus whole
Do not map a constituent work to a trilogy, series, omnibus, or other larger whole merely because the target is contained in it.
Likewise, do not map a whole collection or series to one constituent work.
The granularity of the selected Wikidata item must correspond to the target.

6. Duplicate work-level items
If two or more Wikidata items independently represent the same underlying work, have compatible authorship and title evidence, and neither is clearly an edition, translation, adaptation, or constituent relation, return AMBIGUOUS with the best-supported item as selected_qid and the others as alternatives.

7. Publication year
Treat the supplied year as supporting evidence, not as a hard identity constraint.
It may represent a later edition or contain source metadata errors.
Strong title, author, and work-level identity evidence can override a conflicting year.


ADDITIONAL OPEN LIBRARY EDITION EVIDENCE POLICY

Some packets may contain openlibrary_edition_evidence derived from an
Open Library edition whose normalized title, and usually publication year,
match the target record.

Treat this evidence conservatively.

1. An explicit "translation of X" relation identifies the source work X.
   A translation normally represents the same underlying conceptual work
   for this resolution task. If a candidate clearly represents X, this is
   strong positive evidence. If a candidate represents a different source
   work, do not infer that it is the target merely because author, language,
   or chronology make a translation relationship seem plausible.

2. Explicit "abridged from X", "based on X", or "adapted from X" evidence
   indicates a derivative work relationship. Do not automatically identify
   the derivative target with X itself. Require evidence that the candidate
   represents the derivative target rather than only its source.

3. Responsibility or edition wording such as "edited by", "translated by",
   "adapted and edited by", "revised by", or similar wording does NOT by
   itself establish that the target is a distinct conceptual work.
   Do not reject an otherwise matching underlying work solely because such
   responsibility wording appears in the edition title or statement.

4. work_titles may support interpretation of an explicit relation, but a
   work_titles value alone is not sufficient to determine conceptual-work
   identity when other evidence conflicts.

5. Do not invent source-work relationships that are not present in the
   packet. In particular, do not infer that a differently titled work by
   the same author must be the original of a translation unless explicit
   bibliographic or candidate relation evidence supports that conclusion.
